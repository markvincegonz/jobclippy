## 1. Project Overview

The idea is to create a simple, intelligent tool that helps people organize and manage their job search.

Searching for a job can quickly become overwhelming. A person may find interesting positions on LinkedIn, Indeed, company career pages, startup websites, and many other platforms. Every job has different information, different application requirements, different deadlines, and different stages in the hiring process.

Most people end up managing all of this information manually.

They might:

- Save job links in browser bookmarks.
- Copy job descriptions into Google Docs.
- Keep a spreadsheet of companies.
- Write reminders in their phone.
- Email themselves interesting positions.
- Take screenshots of job postings.
- Forget which jobs they already applied to.
- Forget when an interview is scheduled.
- Lose track of which version of their resume they used.
- Re-read the same job description several times.
- Manually search long job descriptions for salary information and required skills.

The SaaS product solves this problem by turning the entire job search into one organized visual workspace.

The main idea is simple:

> **Find a job → click one button → save it → let the system organize it for you.**

Instead of treating the job search like a collection of random browser tabs, spreadsheets, bookmarks, and notes, the product gives users a single place where every opportunity can be collected, organized, and tracked.

## 2. The Problem

### The modern job search is messy

Imagine someone is looking for a software engineering position.

On Monday morning, they find three interesting jobs on LinkedIn.

They save two of them.

Then they visit a startup's website and find another position.

Later, they discover a fourth position through a friend.

By the end of the day, they have several browser tabs open.

They might remember:

> "I think I applied to that company."

But which company?

What position?

When did they apply?

Which resume did they send?

Was there a salary listed?

Who was the recruiter?

When is the interview?

This becomes even more difficult when someone is applying to 20, 30, 50, or even 100 positions.

A normal spreadsheet can help, but it creates another problem:

**The user has to manually enter everything.**

For every job they find, they may need to type:

- Job title
- Company
- Location
- Salary
- Job description
- Application URL
- Date discovered
- Date applied
- Current status
- Notes
- Skills
- Interview information

That creates friction.

And when a tool creates too much work, people eventually stop using it.

The goal of this SaaS is therefore not simply to create another job tracker.

**The goal is to make tracking a job almost effortless.**

---

## 3. The Big Idea

The product consists of three major parts:

1. **A browser extension** — This lives inside the user's browser and allows them to save a job while they are viewing it.
2. **A visual job-search dashboard** — This is the main website where the user sees all of their saved opportunities.
3. **An intelligent assistant** — This automatically reads the information from saved job postings and organizes important details such as skills and salary.

Together, these three pieces create a simple workflow:

> **Discover → Save → Organize → Apply → Track → Interview → Offer**

The user doesn't have to manually manage every piece of information.

The product does much of the organizational work for them.

## 4. How It Works for the User

Let's imagine a real user.

We'll call her Sarah.

Sarah is a product designer looking for a new job.

She installs the browser extension.

After signing up, she can immediately start saving jobs.

### Step 1: Sarah finds a job

Sarah is browsing LinkedIn.

She sees a position called:

**Senior Product Designer**

at a company she likes.

Normally, she might copy the link into a spreadsheet.

Instead, she simply clicks the extension.

A small window appears.

She clicks:

**Save Job**

That's it.

## 6. The AI Layer

This is where the product becomes more useful than a traditional spreadsheet.

Job descriptions can be extremely long.

A company might write several thousand words describing a position.

The user doesn't necessarily want to read the entire description every time they look at their job tracker.

The system can identify the most important information.

For example, a job description might mention:

- React
- TypeScript
- JavaScript
- Git
- REST APIs
- Agile development
- Team leadership

Instead of forcing the user to remember all of those requirements, the dashboard can display them as simple tags.

For example:

`React` `TypeScript` `JavaScript` `Git` `REST APIs`

This allows the user to understand the general requirements of a job immediately.

---

## 7. Salary Detection

Salary information is another important part of the product.

Many job postings don't put salary information at the top.

Instead, it might be hidden near the bottom of the description.

For example:

> "The expected compensation range for this position is $110,000–$145,000 annually."

The user shouldn't have to search through thousands of words to find that.

The system can identify the salary information and display it prominently.

For example:

**Senior Software Engineer**
**$110K–$145K**

Example Company

Remote

This makes comparing opportunities much easier.

If salary isn't mentioned, the dashboard can simply say:

**Salary not listed**

rather than showing an empty field.

## 8. The Main Dashboard

After Sarah saves several jobs, she opens the SaaS dashboard.

Instead of seeing a boring spreadsheet, she sees a visual board.

The board works similarly to a digital task board.

Each job appears as a card.

The cards can be organized into different stages.

For example:

| Wishlist | Applied | Interviewing | Offer | Archived |
|----------|---------|--------------|-------|----------|
| Company A | Company D | Company G | Company J | Company K |
| Company B | Company E | Company H | | Company L |
| Company C | Company F | | | Company M |

This immediately tells the user what is happening with their job search.

---

## 9. The Job Pipeline

The default stages could be:

### Wishlist

These are jobs the user is interested in but hasn't applied to yet.

For example:

> "This company looks interesting. I want to apply tomorrow."

The user saves it to Wishlist.

### Applied

Once the user submits their application, they move the card into Applied.

Now they can see exactly which positions they've already submitted.

This prevents accidentally applying twice or forgetting about an application.

### Interviewing

If a company contacts the user and schedules an interview, the job moves into Interviewing.

The user can add notes.

For example:

> Interview with Sarah — Tuesday at 2 PM.

Or:

> Technical interview scheduled for Friday.

### Offer Received

If the company makes an offer, the user moves the card into the Offer stage.

This provides a satisfying visual representation of progress.

### Archived / Rejected

Not every application will work out.

Instead of deleting those jobs, the user can move them into an archive.

This keeps the main dashboard clean while preserving the user's history.

## 10. Why the Visual Board Matters

A spreadsheet contains information.

A visual board provides context.

For example, if a user opens the dashboard and sees:

- Wishlist: 18
- Applied: 12
- Interviewing: 4
- Offers: 1

they immediately understand where they are in their search.

They don't have to read rows and rows of spreadsheet data.

The product becomes almost like a **personal command center** for the job hunt.

## 13. Highlight-to-Clip

The browser extension shouldn't only work with major job websites.

There are thousands of company career pages.

Some companies have their own custom websites.

Others use different hiring platforms.

Because of this, the product should have a fallback option.

Imagine the user visits a company website that the extension doesn't automatically recognize.

They can simply highlight the important job information.

For example:

> Senior UX Designer
> Company XYZ
> Remote
> $100,000–$130,000
> We are looking for a designer who...

Then they click the extension and choose:

**Save Highlighted Text**

The system takes the selected information and turns it into a job record.

This makes the product useful across a much wider range of websites.

## 25. The Main MVP

The first version should not try to build everything.

The MVP should focus on one powerful promise:

> **Save jobs instantly and organize your entire job search in one place.**

The MVP can therefore be divided into three major parts.

### MVP Part 1: Browser Extension

The extension should allow users to save jobs.

The initial experience should support:

- LinkedIn job pages
- Highlight-to-save
- Job title detection
- Company detection
- Description capture
- Salary detection
- Link saving

The extension is essentially the entry point into the product.

---

## 26. MVP Part 2: Dashboard

The dashboard should contain:

**Main navigation**

- Dashboard
- All Jobs
- Wishlist
- Applied
- Interviewing
- Offers
- Archived
- Settings

The main dashboard displays the job board.

Users can move jobs between stages.

---

## 27. MVP Part 3: Smart Organization

The system should automatically identify:

- Job title
- Company
- Location
- Salary
- Skills
- Description
- Original URL

The user should be able to edit anything that was captured incorrectly.

This is important because automated systems will occasionally make mistakes.

**The user should always remain in control.**

---

## 28. What the First Version Should NOT Build

To launch quickly, avoid trying to create a massive platform.

The first version probably doesn't need:

- Automatic job applications
- A social network
- A full resume builder
- A complete interview-training platform
- A huge job search engine
- Complex career coaching
- Automated recruiter communication
- Dozens of integrations
- Advanced analytics
- Mobile apps

Those can come later.

The MVP needs to prove one thing:

> **Do people find it valuable to save and organize their jobs this way?**

## 41. Why This Can Become a SaaS

The product has several characteristics that make it suitable for a subscription model.

Users may need it for weeks or months while searching for a job.

During that period, their data becomes increasingly valuable.

The more jobs they save, the more useful their dashboard becomes.

Their history grows.

Their notes grow.

Their interview information grows.

Their application pipeline grows.

Their analytics become more useful.

This creates a reason for users to continue using the product rather than treating it as a one-time tool.

---

## 42. The Most Important Product Principle

The most important principle should be:

> **Never make the user do work that the product can reasonably do for them.**

- If the system can identify the company, don't make the user type the company.
- If it can identify the salary, don't make the user search for it.
- If it can identify the skills, don't make the user manually create every tag.
- If the user can save a job with one click, don't make them fill out a form.

**The product wins by removing friction.**

---

Instead of feeling like they are juggling dozens of job applications, they have a clear visual picture of their entire search.

## Final Product Vision

This SaaS should not try to be "another job board."

It should become the **operating system for someone's job search**.

The product doesn't need to find every job in the world.

It needs to make the jobs the user already finds dramatically easier to manage.

The winning experience is therefore very simple:

> **See a job you like. Save it in one click. Forget about the spreadsheet.**

Everything else — the AI, dashboard, salary detection, skill extraction, reminders, analytics, resume comparison, and interview tools — exists to make that experience increasingly powerful.
